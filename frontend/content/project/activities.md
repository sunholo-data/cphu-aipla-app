# Activities and examples

AIPLA combines a guided AI tutor with interactive learning materials. The tutor can refer to the activity a student is using, ask for predictions, and respond to observations rather than treating the conversation as an isolated chat.

## Paired tutors and simulations

A central AIPLA pattern is to design the tutor and the interactive activity together. A tutor can refer to a particular graph, slider, piece of equipment, or stage in an experiment because those elements are part of its prepared teaching context.

A typical learning sequence is:

1. **Predict** what will happen and explain why.
2. **Explore** by changing a parameter or carrying out a procedure.
3. **Observe** the result in a graph, diagram, measurement, or simulation.
4. **Reflect** with questions from the tutor.
5. **Summarise** the physics in the student's own words.

## Projectile motion

The Boldkast activity pairs a Danish Socratic physics tutor with a projectile-motion simulation. Students can change launch speed, angle, and gravity, then compare the resulting motion and representations.

The tutor uses the student's interaction with the activity as context for its next question. This helps connect equations and graphs with what the student has just observed.

## Planck's constant virtual lab

The LED Planck activity explores a different form of interaction: a procedural virtual laboratory. Students work through a simplified experimental sequence involving an LED circuit, threshold voltage, wavelength, and an estimate of Planck's constant.

This prototype is used to investigate what AI can and cannot contribute to experimental preparation and data interpretation. It complements rather than replaces work with real laboratory equipment.

## Kinematics

KineBot is a kinematics activity with simulations for motion, acceleration, free fall, projectiles, vectors, and graphs. Its migration into AIPLA helped establish a reusable process for bringing externally created teaching activities into the platform safely.

## Learning from AI mistakes

AI-generated explanations and illustrations can be convincing while still being physically wrong. AIPLA treats these failures as possible teaching material: students can compare a plausible misconception with a correct physical account and explain the difference.

The purpose is not to celebrate incorrect output, but to help students learn to inspect representations critically and recognise where physical reasoning matters.
